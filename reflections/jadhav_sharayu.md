## Paper 1: Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate

**1. Full Citation & Link**

Du, Y., Li, S., Torralba, A., Tenenbaum, J. B., & Mordatch, I. (2023).
*Improving factuality and reasoning in language models through
multiagent debate.* arXiv.\
https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325

**2. Structured Summary**

Du et al. (2023) investigate whether multiple language-model instances
can improve reasoning by examining and challenging one another's answers
instead of relying on a single model response. Their approach has
several model instances independently generate answers and reasoning,
then participate in multiple rounds of debate in which each model can
reconsider its response after seeing the reasoning produced by the
others. The authors evaluate this method across mathematical reasoning,
strategic reasoning, and factual-generation tasks. Their experiments
show that multi-agent debate can improve reasoning performance and the
factual validity of generated responses while reducing some hallucinated
or incorrect answers. The paper therefore provides evidence that
interaction among multiple model instances can be useful as an
inference-time reasoning strategy rather than simply accepting the first
output generated by one model.

**3. Three Key Insights**

1.  A single LLM response does not have to be treated as the final
    answer. Allowing multiple model instances to expose and challenge
    different reasoning paths can help uncover mistakes that might
    otherwise remain unnoticed.
2.  Multi-agent systems are valuable not simply because they contain
    more agents, but because the agents exchange information and
    reconsider their reasoning. The interaction between agents is
    therefore an important part of the architecture.
3.  The paper demonstrates improvements across several different tasks
    using the same general debate procedure, suggesting that multi-agent
    reasoning can function as a general inference strategy rather than
    being limited to one narrow application.

**4. Two Limitations or Risks**

1.  The paper studies debate between multiple instances of language
    models on benchmark reasoning and factuality tasks. ReturnGuard
    instead uses specialized components with different responsibilities,
    such as policy retrieval, image analysis, behavioral analysis, and
    decision synthesis. Therefore, the paper supports the broader idea
    of multi-agent reasoning but does not directly validate
    ReturnGuard's exact architecture.
2.  Adding multiple agents and rounds of interaction increases the
    number of model calls and therefore potentially increases latency
    and computational cost. A more complicated multi-agent system should
    consequently be compared against a simpler single-agent baseline
    rather than assuming that additional agents automatically produce a
    better system.

**5. One Concrete Inspiration**

This paper directly inspired ReturnGuard's use of multiple reasoning
components rather than asking one LLM to inspect a return and
immediately make a recommendation. Policy, image, and behavioral
evidence can be analyzed separately and then brought together by the
Decision component. It also motivates our Checkpoint 2 experiment
comparing ReturnGuard's multi-agent approach against a single-LLM
baseline on the same return scenarios, so we can test whether the added
architecture actually improves evidence-grounded assessment.

## Paper 2: CRITIC: Large Language Models Can Self-Correct with Tool-Interactive Critiquing

**1. Full Citation & Link**

Gou, Z., Shao, Z., Gong, Y., Shen, Y., Yang, Y., Duan, N., & Chen, W.
(2024). *CRITIC: Large language models can self-correct with
tool-interactive critiquing.* International Conference on Learning
Representations (ICLR 2024).\
https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738

**2. Structured Summary**

Gou et al. introduce CRITIC, a framework designed to improve LLM outputs
through external feedback rather than relying entirely on the model's
internal knowledge to identify its own mistakes. The system first
generates an initial response, interacts with appropriate external tools
to evaluate aspects of that response, and then uses the resulting
feedback to critique and revise its output. The authors evaluate the
framework on tasks including question answering, mathematical program
synthesis, and toxicity reduction. Their experiments show that
tool-interactive critiquing can improve model performance across these
settings and highlight the importance of external feedback during
self-correction. The work therefore suggests that verification grounded
in external evidence can be more useful than simply asking a model to
reconsider its answer without giving it additional information.

**3. Three Key Insights**

1.  Asking an LLM to "check its answer" is different from giving it
    external information with which to perform that check. CRITIC shows
    the value of grounding critique in feedback obtained from tools
    rather than relying only on the model's own internal reasoning.
2.  Generation and verification can be treated as separate stages. An
    initial answer does not have to be immediately accepted; it can
    first pass through an explicit critique-and-revision process.
3.  External feedback creates an opportunity to catch problems such as
    factual errors or flawed reasoning before an output is presented as
    final, which is especially relevant when AI outputs influence
    consequential decisions.

**4. Two Limitations or Risks**

1.  CRITIC's effectiveness depends on the quality of the external tools
    and the feedback they provide. If a tool returns incomplete or
    incorrect information, the critique may reinforce an error rather
    than correct it.
2.  The framework improves outputs in the tasks evaluated by the
    authors, but it does not guarantee that every incorrect output will
    be detected. A critic component should therefore be treated as an
    additional verification layer rather than proof that the final
    recommendation is correct.

**5. One Concrete Inspiration**

This paper directly motivates ReturnGuard's **Critic** stage. After the
Decision component synthesizes evidence from Policy RAG, image analysis,
and behavioral signals, the recommendation should not immediately become
the system's final output. A Critic component can re-check whether the
recommendation is supported by the available evidence and identify
inconsistencies before the Explanation and Governance stages. This gives
ReturnGuard an explicit verification step rather than trusting the
Decision component's first answer.
